We measure admin in keystrokes because a keystroke can be counted and an hour
can only be remembered. A keystroke, as we use the word, is one thing a person
has to type or click to move the work along: a character, a click, a copy, a
paste, a file attached. We count them from a recording of the job, then
multiply by how often the business's own records say the job happens. The
full rules are on our [counting page](/counting).

Ask someone how long the month-end takes and you get a number that sounds
right. Record them doing it and count, and you get a number you can check.

## An hour is a memory

People are poor witnesses to their own working time. In a
[study of 1,211 people who worked at computers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2078486),
their own estimates of keyboard and mouse time explained only a quarter to a
third of what software recorded over the same four weeks, and they tended to
overestimate. Predictions fare no better: people asked how long a task will
take are reliably optimistic, a habit researchers call the
[planning fallacy](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/heuristics-and-biases/inside-the-planning-fallacy-the-causes-and-consequences-of-optimistic-time-predictions/CA157EF59A218C2B906A1795089E7650).

Even the best-known figures on small business admin are estimates. In ACCI's
[2025 Small Business Conditions Report](https://acci.com.au/Common/Uploaded%20files/Smart%20Suite/Smart%20Library/438ed93b-de8a-4fe2-896e-9d5ba2dc7655/Small%20Business%20Conditions%20Survey%20(9).pdf),
39% of owners and managers said they spend more than six hours a week on red
tape. That may well be right, but it is a number people gave about
themselves, and nobody counted it.

Hours also hide what is in them. "Two hours on invoicing" could be two hours
of careful work or two hours of typing the same customer's details into three
systems. A count separates the two, because only the inputs a rebuilt system
would no longer need are counted. Reading, deciding and checking are not.

## A keystroke is what automation removes

Nobody automates an hour. What gets automated is a particular input: the
address typed a second time, the job number copied into the invoice, the
figure pasted from one spreadsheet into another. So we count the job in the
same unit before and after it is rebuilt, and the difference is exactly what
the build took away.

Where admin is slow, it is usually because the same facts are entered more
than once, in systems that do not talk to each other: the customer in the job
system, again in the accounts, and again in the spreadsheet someone keeps on
the side. A count shows each copy as its own set of keystrokes.

## Every keystroke typed twice is a chance to get it wrong

Each time a fact is typed again, it can be typed wrong. A
[review of 93 studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10775420/) of
clinical research data found a pooled error rate of 0.29% for data keyed in
once, and 6.57% when people copied details by hand out of medical records.
That is a careful field that checks its data. A small error rate on a detail
typed thousands of times a year still makes a steady supply of wrong addresses
and mistyped amounts, and each one costs more to find later than it did to
type.

## Small jobs add up

A job that takes a few minutes and happens dozens of times a week matters more
than a job that takes a day and happens once a year. A count makes that
visible, because it is multiplied by the job's real frequency, taken from the
business's own records rather than from what anyone remembers. The
[counting page](/counting) works one through: invoicing that runs 60 times a
week and clears 32 inputs each time takes 1,920 keystrokes a week off someone.

## Where the idea comes from

Counting the actions in a job is an old idea in computing. In 1980 Stuart
Card, Thomas Moran and Allen Newell published the Keystroke-Level Model, which
predicts how long a practised person takes to do a routine task: list every
keystroke, mouse movement and pause to think, then add up a standard time for
each. David Kieras of the University of Michigan sets out the
[method and its times](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~golbeck/INST631/KSM.pdf) in a
short guide.

We use the counting half of that idea. Our count records what a person had to
do, which is the part a rebuild can change, and we leave the timing to the
person who does the job.

## What the count leaves out

A keystroke count is not a measure of how hard a job is, or how much it
matters. It leaves out things that cost a business real time:

- Reading, deciding and checking. We do not count them, and a good rebuild
  usually leaves them with a person.
- Waiting: for an approval, for a reply, for someone to come back from leave.
- Switching between jobs, and the time it takes to get going again.

Switching has been studied closely. In
[a study of office workers](https://www.interruptions.net/literature/Mark-CHI05-p321-mark.pdf)
by Gloria Mark and colleagues at the University of California, Irvine, people
spent about 11 minutes on a piece of work before they switched or were
interrupted, and 57% of those pieces of work were interrupted. A rebuild that
removes a copy-and-paste job also removes a reason to stop and switch to it.
We do not count that, so the figure we report is the smaller one.

We also keep the count honest at the other end. A job counts as cleared only
after the rebuilt version has run for four weeks with nobody stepping in to do
it by hand. A job that is half automated counts for nothing. We do not publish
an industry average, and we do not turn keystrokes into dollars in anything
we write.

## What a count shows you

The [Keystroke Audit](/audit) is a count of one job. Someone does it once,
the usual way, while we record it. We count every keystroke in it and how
often it happens in a year, and the report says what it costs in hours, what
to fix first and what the fix would cost. If the number turns out to be small,
the report says so, and you can stop there.
