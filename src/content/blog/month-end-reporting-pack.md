A month-end reporting pack is the few pages that tell the owner how last
month went and what to do about it. For a small business it needs seven
pages, read in this order: the month on one page, profit against budget and
last year, cash now and over the quarter ahead, who owes you and who you owe,
profit by job or customer, the balance sheet, and the actions. Everything else
is detail for whoever asks.

Most guides to the month-end pack are written for a finance team reporting to
a board. A business with a handful of people, a Xero or MYOB file and a job
system needs a shorter pack, built from the same systems every month, and
ready while the month can still be acted on.

## First, the month has to be closed

A pack built on unreconciled figures is a guess with a logo on it. Before
anything goes in the pack, the month needs closing: bank and card accounts
reconciled, bills and invoices entered, and payroll checked. Xero's guide to
the [month-end close](https://www.xero.com/au/guides/month-end-close-process/)
puts it at three to five days for many small businesses, and five to ten
where there is stock, more than one entity or a lot of transactions.

Payroll matters more than it did. From 1 July 2026 employers pay super at the
same time as wages, and it generally has to reach the fund within 7 days of
payday, as [business.gov.au](https://business.gov.au/news/changes-for-businesses-from-1-july-2026)
sets out. Check the month's pay runs against what was reported through Single
Touch Payroll and what was paid to super funds before the pack goes out.

## The pack, page by page

Each page answers one question the owner asks: how did we go, have we got the
cash, who owes us, which jobs made money, and what are we doing about it. A
page that answers no one's question is padding.

1. **The month on one page.** Three to six numbers, each beside last year's
   figure and the target, and three lines of plain English on what moved them.
   If the owner reads nothing else, this page should be enough to decide what
   to look at.
2. **Profit and loss.** This month and the year to date, against budget and
   against the same month last year. Show the difference in dollars and as a
   percentage, and write one line on any line that moved more than you would
   expect.
3. **Cash.** The bank balance on the last day of the month, and a forecast of
   what goes in and out over the quarter ahead, week by week: wages,
   super, rent, the BAS, loan repayments and the invoices you expect to be
   paid.
4. **Who owes you, and who you owe.** Aged debtors and aged creditors, with the
   overdue amounts at the top and a name against each one being chased.
5. **Profit by job or customer.** For a trade or service business this is the
   page that changes decisions. It comes from the job system: quoted against
   actual hours and materials, job by job.
6. **The balance sheet.** One page. Point to working capital: what is owed to
   you, what you owe, stock and cash.
7. **What we are doing about it.** Each action with a person and a date. Next
   month's pack opens by saying what happened to them.

Profit comes before cash because it explains the month, and
cash comes before the balance sheet because it decides the quarter ahead. Most packs put the balance sheet second because the accounting
software prints it second.

## Which numbers go on the first page

Pick no more than six, and keep the same ones every month so the trend means
something. For most small businesses these are enough to start with:

- Revenue against the same month last year.
- Gross margin, as a percentage.
- Cash at the bank, and how many weeks of costs it covers.
- Debtor days: how long customers take to pay.
- The amount more than a month overdue.
- One number from the work itself, such as jobs completed, hours billed or
  quotes won.

Xero's guide to [management accounts](https://www.xero.com/uk/guides/management-accounts/)
suggests a similar starting set: gross margin, operating margin, debtor days,
creditor days and cash conversion. Whatever you choose, show each number
against something: last year, the budget or a target. A number on its own does
not tell anyone whether to act.

## Why cash gets its own page

Profit and cash part ways every month: a job invoiced is profit, but it is not
in the bank until the customer pays. In ASIC's [insolvency figures for 2022–23](https://asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2023-releases/23-346mr-asic-s-annual-corporate-insolvency-statistics-shows-covid-19-impact-on-small-business),
inadequate cash flow or high cash use was the most common cause of failure
named by administrators, in 52% of their reports, and 82% of the failed
companies had fewer than 20 employees.

The aged debtors page is where cash goes missing. In the September quarter
of 2026, Xero's [small business data](https://www.xero.com/au/resources/small-business-insights/latest-australia/)
showed Australian small businesses waiting 24.3 days on average to be paid,
and being paid 6.9 days late. A pack that lists the overdue invoices by name
gets them chased the same morning.

CPA Australia's [checklist for small business](https://www.cpaaustralia.com.au/-/media/project/cpa/corporate/documents/tools-and-resources/business-management/good-practice-checklist-small-business.pdf)
asks for the same rhythm: review results against budget monthly, update the
cash flow forecast monthly, and reconcile the debtors' ageing report at least
monthly.

## When the pack should be ready

A pack is worth most in the first week of the month. By the third week, the
owner has already made the decisions it was meant to inform, from memory and
the bank balance. If the close takes three to five days, the pack should be
read by the end of the first week.

What holds most packs up is copying: exporting from the accounts and the job
system, pasting into a spreadsheet, fixing the formulas that broke, and
formatting the same pages again. When the pack is built from the systems
directly, the close is the only work left: when the books are closed, the pack
is ready. The packs we build read straight from Xero or MYOB and arrive as a
Power BI report and a PDF emailed to the owner, with a one-page guide to
reading it.

## A monthly pack when the BAS is quarterly

Most small businesses lodge their BAS quarterly; only very large businesses
have to lodge monthly, as
[business.gov.au](https://business.gov.au/finance/yearly-financial-tasks/important-financial-dates)
sets out. That does not make monthly reporting optional. The BAS tells the ATO
what you owe. The pack tells you how the business is going, and a business
that reconciles every month finds the BAS a short job at the end of the
quarter.

## Questions people ask

### What is the difference between a month-end pack and the annual accounts?

The annual accounts are prepared once a year, mostly for tax and for lenders,
and they arrive months after the year they describe. The month-end pack is for
running the business: it is shorter, it compares against the budget, and it
arrives while there is still time to act on it.

### Can Xero or MYOB produce the pack on its own?

Both print the profit and loss, the balance sheet and aged receivables and
payables. Neither puts them in one pack beside your job figures, with targets
and the actions from last month. That last step is usually a spreadsheet
someone rebuilds every month, and it is the part worth automating.

### How many pages should it be?

Seven, as above, and the first one does most of the work. If a page has not
changed a decision in three months, take it out.
